    The setting

    Welcome to Meridian

    Meridian is a fictional coastal city-region of 1.6 million people, which is expected to grow to 1.9 million by 2035. A compact centre and an older inner ring sit beside a busy container port, whilst most residents live in car-dependent middle and outer suburbs, where three in four trips are made by car. Further, the electricity grid is becoming cleaner, albeit slowly, summers are getting hotter, and the river has flooded twice in the past ten years.

    You act as the combined state and local government. If nothing changes, Meridian is expected to emit 100 CU (carbon units) in 2035, that is, 45 from transport, 25 from electricity, 15 from buildings, 10 from construction and industry, and 5 from waste and land.

    Your mission

    1. Carbon. Cut at least 40 CU, so that emissions in 2035 are 60 CU or less.
    2. Money. Spend no more than 16 coins over ten years. Pricing cards raise revenue and, consequently, show a negative cost.
    3. Two criteria. Choose two of Equity, Resilience, Health and safety, and Liveability, and lift each of them to +7 or more.
    4. Do no significant harm. None of the four criteria may fall below 0.
    5. Constraints. Respect the two constraint cards dealt to your group.

    Synergies

      Revenue recycling

      For the Congestion charge and the Road user charge, the revenue may be returned to low-income households. In so doing, the card costs one extra coin, although its Equity score becomes +1.

      Lead time

      This is the number of years needed before a measure takes full effect. The CU reported on each card already account for it, and slow cards also report what they would add after 2035.

      Take-home resources

      These resources come from the ATRF 2026 workshop led by Dr Andrea Pellegrini and Professor John M. Rose (Institute of Transport and Logistics Studies, The University of Sydney Business School). The no-regrets list and the roadmap developed by the room will be added after the session.

      1. A working definition

      A sustainable future is one in which we cut emissions fast enough to stay within our carbon budget, in ways that are fair, keep people healthy and safe, hold up under shocks, and that people can afford and are willing to support.

      This draft was brought to the room and edited live. It builds on the idea, set out in the Brundtland report, of meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own (World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987).

      2. The 5C test for any package of measures

      C1Carbon

      Does the package cut enough, and early enough? Given that cumulative carbon dioxide emissions largely determine warming, a tonne saved in 2027 counts for more than one promised for 2040.

      C2Criteria

      Which two outcomes, beyond emissions, must the package deliver? A bar should be set for each of them, and it should be checked that no significant harm is done to the others.

      C3Constraints

      Does the package still work when money, politics, the workforce, the grid or supply chains become binding? It should be tested under at least two of these.

      C4Combinations

      Which measures need each other? A push needs a pull, new homes need transit, and electric vehicles need a clean grid.

      C5Consent

      Who gains, who pays, and how is the revenue returned? Further, is there a trial that may allow people to see the benefits before they are asked to vote on it?

      3. Sorting the moves

      Before the workshop, each of the roughly 16.8 million possible portfolios was checked against the constraints of every group. The pattern below summarises what this search found, and it may be compared with the choices made in the room.

      No-regrets

      These measures appear in more than half of the winning portfolios under the constraints of every group: the parking levy, homes near stations, low-carbon procurement, turn-up-and-go frequency, the hybrid work deal and safer speeds.

      Conditional

      The congestion charge and the road user charge are powerful, although only where politics allows them. They appear in almost every winning portfolio that is permitted to use them.

      The long game

      The metro line and 20-minute neighbourhoods are worth starting now, despite most of their benefits arriving after 2035. Homes near stations also belong here, which partly explains why they are a no-regrets move as well.

      Regret risk

      The motorway upgrade locks in both emissions and money. It appears in winning portfolios almost only where a minister has insisted on it.

      About the game

      Meridian, its numbers and its cards were created for teaching purposes. Card values are relative and simplified, so that a group can build a portfolio in about 20 minutes. The real-world examples on the cards indicate where a similar measure has been tried, not that it produced the same effect. Of further note is that this page requires no account and sends nothing anywhere, that is, all choices remain on your own phone.
